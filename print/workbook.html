Welcome

This guide is for adults who have been feeling low, stressed, tired or stuck for a while. It teaches practical skills that research has shown can lift mood and make daily life feel more manageable.

There are five sessions. Each takes about 30 minutes. We suggest one session a week, with small things to practise in between. The practice is where most of the change happens.

Before you begin

  • This is a self-help guide. It does not replace a doctor, nurse or counsellor.
  • Your answers are saved only on this device. Nobody else can see them unless you show them.
  • If at any point you feel unsafe or think about ending your life, stop and open Urgent help. You deserve support straight away.

Amara: I used to love cooking for people and going out on Sundays. Now I mostly stay inside. When people call, I let the phone ring.
Ada: Thank you for telling me, Amara. When people go through stress, loss or long periods of difficulty, it is very common to feel like this. It's not weakness and it's not your fault.

Signs of low mood

Feeling low for a long time can affect your whole body and life. You might notice:

  • Feeling sad, empty or hopeless
  • Losing interest in things you enjoyed
  • Tiredness and low energy
  • Sleeping too much or too little
  • Eating more or less than usual
  • Finding it hard to concentrate
  • Feeling worthless or guilty
  • Aches, pains or headaches
  • Staying away from people
  • Feeling irritable or tearful

You don't need to have all of these. Most people have a few.

Ada: There's a pattern that keeps low mood going. Let me show you.

The low mood cycle

Feel low → Do less → Fewer good moments, more problems → Feel lower → …

When we feel low, we naturally do less. We rest more, see people less and put tasks off. That makes sense — we're tired.

But doing less means fewer good moments, fewer things achieved and more problems piling up. So we feel even lower, and do even less.

The good news: the cycle can also turn the other way. Small actions bring small good moments, which give a little more energy for the next step.

Amara: But I don't feel like doing anything. Shouldn't I wait until I feel better?
Ada: That's what most people think. But with low mood, waiting rarely works. Action usually comes first — and the better feeling follows later. We start with steps so small they're hard to say no to.

Two kinds of helpful activity

Enjoyable

Things that give you a moment of pleasure or calm: music, a walk, a favourite meal, sitting outside, a chat.

Getting things done

Things that give a sense of achievement: washing clothes, fixing something, paying a bill, cooking.

Both lift mood. A good week has a little of each.

When a task feels too big, make it smaller

Big tasks feel impossible when we're low. Break them into steps, and do just the first one.

  1. Write a short list of what to buy
  2. Go to the market for those items
  3. Wash and cut the vegetables
  4. Cook
  5. Wash up — or leave it for the morning

Each step done is a success, even if you stop there.

Amara: Another thing: sometimes before going out, my heart races, my chest feels tight and my mind won't stop. Then I don't go.
Ada: That is your body's stress reaction. It's uncomfortable, but not dangerous. A simple way to calm it is slow breathing. Let's try it together now.

Slow breathing

  1. Sit comfortably with your feet on the floor. Rest your hands in your lap.
  2. Breathe out fully, as if letting go of everything.
  3. Breathe in gently through your nose and let your belly rise, slowly counting 1, 2, 3, 4.
  4. Breathe out slowly through your mouth, counting 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.
  5. Keep going for about two minutes. If your mind wanders, gently come back to counting.

If you feel dizzy, breathe normally for a moment, then continue more gently.

✎ Try it now. Follow the circle: breathe in as it grows, out as it shrinks.

Amara: I went to the market and cooked! But then I burned the stew a bit and thought, "I can't even do this properly. I'm useless."
Ada: You went to the market and cooked a meal — two things you couldn't face last week. And yet the voice in your head called you useless. Let's look at that voice.

The harsh inner voice

When we feel low, our thoughts often become harsh and unfair. Common patterns:

  • All-or-nothing: "If it isn't perfect, it's a failure."
  • Harsh labels: "I'm useless." "I'm a burden."
  • Expecting the worst: "It will go badly, so why try?"
  • Ignoring the good: "That doesn't count."

These thoughts feel true, but they are a symptom of low mood — not facts about you.

Ada: Here's a useful question: if a good friend burned their stew, would you call them useless?
Amara: No… I'd say, "Well done for cooking — it still tastes good."

Positive self-talk

Positive self-talk isn't pretending everything is fine. It means speaking to yourself the way a kind, fair friend would.

Harsh"I'm useless."
Kinder"I'm finding things hard right now, but I cooked today. That's a step."
Harsh"Nobody wants to see me."
Kinder"I don't know that. Some people have asked about me."
Harsh"This will never get better."
Kinder"It's hard now. I've already made small changes, and I can keep going."

Ada: Many people feel that way. But being with others — even briefly — is one of the strongest protections for our mood. And support isn't only about talking about problems.

Different kinds of support

Company

Simply being with someone: sharing a meal, watching a match, sitting together.

Listening

Someone you can talk to honestly about how you feel.

Practical help

Help with tasks: childcare, a lift, borrowing something.

Advice and information

Someone who knows about jobs, health, services or your situation.

Support can come from family, friends, neighbours, colleagues, a faith community, community groups, or health and social workers.

Ada: That's understandable. You don't need to explain everything. A short message is enough to start — something like, "I've been thinking of you. How are you?"

Reconnecting, step by step

  1. Choose one person from your list.
  2. Pick a small way to make contact: a text, a short call, a greeting at church or mosque, a visit.
  3. Decide what you'll say. Keep it simple.
  4. Use slow breathing beforehand if you feel nervous.
  5. Afterwards, notice how you feel — and be kind to yourself whatever happens.
